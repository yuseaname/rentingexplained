## Answer body (240 words)

What you're describing has a legal name: **retaliation** — and it's illegal in every US state, though the specifics vary.

**The general rule:**
- A landlord cannot raise rent, evict you, or non-renew your lease in retaliation for exercising a legal right (filing a complaint, requesting repairs, joining a tenant union, reporting code violations).

**Examples of protected activity:**
- Reporting a health or safety violation to a government agency
- Withholding rent (where legal)
- Organizing or joining a tenant union
- Filing a complaint with a housing authority
- Suing or threatening to sue the landlord

**Examples of likely retaliation:**
- Sudden rent increase within 90 days of a complaint
- Eviction notice shortly after a repair request
- Non-renewal shortly after a complaint
- Sudden service reductions (parking spot, laundry access)

**What to do:**

1. **Document the timeline.** Dates of complaints, dates of landlord's actions, exact dollar amounts.
2. **Send a written notice** citing your state's anti-retaliation statute. Most states put the burden on the landlord to prove the rent increase was NOT retaliatory.
3. **File a complaint with your local housing authority or state attorney general.**
4. **Talk to a tenant union or legal aid** — most will take retaliation cases because they have a strong record of winning.

**Strongest retaliation protections:**
- California (CC §1942.5)
- New York (RPL §226-b)
- Illinois (Chicago RLTO §5-12-150)
- Massachusetts (Ch. 186 §18)
- New Jersey (NJSA 2A:42-10.1)

**Damages** can include the rent differential, attorney's fees, and in some states, 2–3× the actual damages.
