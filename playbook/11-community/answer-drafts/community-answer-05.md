## Answer body (320 words)

Whether 3 months is enforceable depends on your **state** and your **lease clauses**, but in most states landlords have a **duty to mitigate** — meaning they must try to re-rent the unit and can't just charge you for empty months.

**Key rule (most states):**
> A landlord cannot just keep collecting your rent while the unit sits empty. They must make reasonable efforts to find a new tenant.

**What to do:**

**Step 1 — Read your lease's "early termination" clause.**
Many leases have a buyout fee (often 1–2 months rent). If that's there, it's usually enforceable as liquidated damages. But 3 months is aggressive unless the lease explicitly states it.

**Step 2 — Negotiate.**
Most landlords will accept 1–2 months rent + a clean re-rent. Offer to:
- Help find a replacement tenant (sublet or assign).
- Keep paying until they re-rent.
- Pay a buyout fee of 1 month.

**Step 3 — Document your cooperation in writing.**
If the landlord refuses to mitigate (i.e., refuses to show the unit, refuses to accept a qualified replacement), they may have waived their right to collect from you.

**Step 4 — Understand your state law.**
- **California, New York, Massachusetts, Maryland, New Jersey**: explicit duty to mitigate.
- **Texas, Florida, Georgia**: implied duty to mitigate.
- **Arkansas**: no duty to mitigate (rare, but real).

**Step 5 — If the landlord refuses:**
- Send a written notice citing your state's duty to mitigate.
- File in small claims court (most states have a $5K–$10K limit).
- Get free help from a tenant union or legal aid.

**Bottom line:** In most states, you owe actual damages (lost rent + re-renting costs) — not 3 months rent as punishment. The landlord's duty is to mitigate.
