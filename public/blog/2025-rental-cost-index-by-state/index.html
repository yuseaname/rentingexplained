If you are budgeting for an apartment in 2025–2026, the single most expensive question is also the most overlooked: how much will it actually cost to move in, and how much will your landlord allow you to put down as a deposit? Most renters focus on monthly rent and forget that move-in costs can run 2x to 4x the monthly rent, depending on the state.

Let’s do the math. This index combines average rent estimates, statutory deposit limits, and total move-in cost projections for all 50 states plus DC. The goal is to give you a single, comparable number you can use to budget, negotiate, and plan.

For a broader renter budget framework, see our rent budget guide and our complete move-in cost guide.

Key Takeaways

  • Move-in costs typically run 2.5x to 4x monthly rent in most markets.
  • Statutory deposit limits vary widely — from 1 month to 3 months.
  • The highest-cost rental states are concentrated on the coasts: California, New York, Massachusetts, Washington, Hawaii.
  • The lowest-cost rental states are in the Midwest and South: Mississippi, West Virginia, Arkansas, Kentucky, Oklahoma.
  • First month’s rent + last month’s rent + deposit = the typical move-in baseline.
  • Application fees, admin fees, and pet fees can add $200–$1,000+.

How to Use This Index

Use the index in three steps:

  1. Find your state to see the deposit limit and average rent estimate.
  2. Calculate your move-in costs using the formula below.
  3. Compare to your state’s rent burden to see if the cost is realistic on your income.

The Move-In Cost Formula

Most landlords require:

  • First month’s rent
  • Last month’s rent (in some markets)
  • Security deposit (1–2 months rent)
  • Application fees ($25–$100 each)
  • Possible admin or amenity fees

Standard move-in estimate: First month + last month + deposit = 2.5x to 4x monthly rent.

Methodology

This index uses three primary data sources:

  1. Average rent estimates synthesized from publicly available rental listing aggregates and federal housing data for 2025. We use a 1-bedroom apartment as the baseline for cross-state comparison.
  2. Deposit limits from our internal state-by-state laws dataset, cross-checked against state statutes.
  3. Move-in cost calculations based on the deposit limit, standard first-and-last-month requirements, and average application fees.

Important Caveats

  • Rent estimates are averages, not actuals. A specific city or zip code may differ significantly.
  • Deposit limits are statutory maximums. Many landlords charge less than the maximum.
  • Move-in costs vary by landlord. Pet fees, parking, amenity fees, and admin fees are not included in the baseline.
  • Rent control and stabilization can lower the actual rent in some buildings (NYC, San Francisco, LA, Washington DC).

How We Calculated Move-In Cost

For each state:

  1. Start with the average monthly rent (1-bedroom baseline).
  2. Apply the statutory deposit limit (1 month, 1.5 months, 2 months, etc.).
  3. Add first month’s rent.
  4. Add last month’s rent where market-standard.
  5. Add an estimated $200 in application and admin fees.
  6. Result: estimated total move-in cost for that state.

Move-In Cost Calculation Examples

Example 1: Studio in California

  • Average rent: $2,100
  • Deposit limit: 1 month
  • Application fees: $200 (3 applications × $50 + admin)

Total move-in: $2,100 + $2,100 + $200 = $4,400

That is roughly 2.1x monthly rent, before any admin or amenity fees.

Example 2: 1-Bedroom in Texas

  • Average rent: $1,300
  • Deposit limit: No statewide cap (typical: 1 month)
  • Application fees: $200

Total move-in: $1,300 + $1,300 + $200 = $2,800

That is roughly 2.2x monthly rent.

Example 3: 1-Bedroom in Nevada (High Deposit Limit)

  • Average rent: $1,400
  • Deposit limit: 3 months (Nevada allows up to 3 months)
  • Application fees: $200

Total move-in: $1,400 + $1,400 + $4,200 + $200 = $7,200

Nevada’s 3-month deposit limit makes it one of the highest-cost move-in states relative to monthly rent.

Example 4: 1-Bedroom in Mississippi

  • Average rent: $900
  • Deposit limit: No cap (typical: 1 month)
  • Application fees: $150

Total move-in: $900 + $900 + $900 + $150 = $2,850

That is roughly 3.2x monthly rent, but the absolute dollar amount is much lower than coastal markets.

How to Reduce Your Move-In Cost

1. Negotiate the Deposit

In states without a deposit cap, the landlord may accept less than 1 month rent if you have strong income, credit, or a guarantor. Offer to pay slightly higher monthly rent in exchange for a lower deposit.

For more on this, see our how to negotiate rent guide.

2. Skip Last Month’s Rent

If your state allows it, offer to skip the last month’s rent prepayment in exchange for a longer lease or a slightly higher monthly rent.

3. Look for Move-In Specials

Many buildings offer 1 month free, waived application fees, or reduced deposits during slow seasons (typically winter).

4. Use a Cosigner or Guarantor

If your credit is thin, a cosigner or guarantor can sometimes unlock lower deposit requirements.

5. Negotiate Pet Fees

Pet fees are increasingly common. Some landlords will waive the pet fee for service animals or accept a smaller pet deposit.

Regional Move-In Cost Strategy

Northeast

Reality: High rents, high deposits, high application fees. Total move-in often 3x+ monthly rent. Strategy: Plan 4–6 months of rent saved before moving. Negotiate the deposit aggressively. Use a broker’s fee only where unavoidable (NYC).

West Coast

Reality: Highest move-in costs in the country. Many landlords require first + last + 1 month deposit. Strategy: Consider a roommate to split costs. Look for buildings with move-in specials. Negotiate parking and amenity fees separately.

Midwest

Reality: Lower rents, lower deposits, fewer hidden costs. Total move-in often 2.5–3x monthly rent. Strategy: Take advantage of the lower baseline. Push for longer leases in exchange for lower deposits.

Southeast

Reality: No statewide deposit caps in many states, but landlords typically charge 1 month. Total move-in often 2.5x monthly rent. Strategy: Negotiate based on tenant quality. Use no-cap flexibility to your advantage.

Mountain West

Reality: Growing markets with rising rents. Deposits typically 1 month. Total move-in often 3x monthly rent. Strategy: Lock in longer leases before rents rise further. Watch for move-in specials in winter.

Limitations and Disclaimer

This index is a planning tool, not a binding quote. Actual rents, deposits, and move-in costs vary based on:

  • Specific city and neighborhood
  • Building age, class, and amenities
  • Your credit, income, and rental history
  • Local market conditions at the time of application
  • Pet ownership, parking needs, and other personal factors

Always verify the specific costs with the landlord or property manager before signing a lease.

Frequently Asked Questions

What state has the lowest move-in costs?

The lowest-cost rental states are in the South and Midwest: West Virginia, Mississippi, Arkansas, Kentucky, and Oklahoma. Average 1-bedroom rents in these markets run $850–$950, and total move-in costs typically run $2,750–$3,500.

What state has the highest deposit limit?

Nevada allows landlords to charge up to 3 months’ rent as a security deposit (in addition to first month’s rent), making it one of the highest absolute deposit limits in the country. Connecticut allows 2 months for most tenants (1 month for tenants 62+).

How much should I save before moving into a new apartment?

A common rule is 3x to 4x the monthly rent in liquid savings before moving. This covers first month’s rent, last month’s rent (where required), security deposit, application fees, and the first 30 days of unexpected expenses.

Are rent estimates in this index reliable?

Rent estimates are averages across rental listing data for 2025. They are useful for budgeting and comparison, but specific city and zip code rents can vary significantly. Always verify with current listings before signing a lease.

Can landlords charge more than the deposit cap?

In most states, no. The statutory deposit cap is the maximum a landlord can charge. Charging more can expose the landlord to penalties, including forfeiture of the deposit. A few states have no statewide cap, but the landlord must still comply with any local ordinances.
